Finding the **most fuel-efficient petrol car** has become a key purchasing criterion, between rising pump prices and driving restrictions that increasingly target older engines. The consumption gap between two petrol models can exceed 3 litres per 100 kilometres, a figure that weighs directly on the annual fuel budget. Here is the ranking of the most economical models, the criteria behind these gaps, and practical ways to cut your own fuel consumption day to day.

## What makes a petrol car use less fuel

The fuel consumption of a petrol engine depends first on the vehicle's weight and frontal area, two factors that determine the energy needed to overcome inertia and air resistance. For the same engine size, a lightweight city car structurally consumes less than a compact car or an SUV, which explains why small vehicles dominate every fuel-efficiency ranking.

The second decisive factor is the type of powertrain. A non-plug-in hybrid petrol engine recovers energy under braking and drives in all-electric mode at low speed, which significantly cuts urban consumption compared with a conventional petrol engine of equivalent power. Recent small turbocharged three-cylinder engines, often paired with cylinder deactivation, also strike a good balance between driving pleasure and fuel economy, without the extra cost of full hybridisation.

Driving style and trip profile complete the picture. Mostly urban use with frequent stops favours hybrids, while steady motorway driving reduces the hybrid advantage and narrows the gap between conventional petrol and hybrid engines.

Air conditioning and onboard electrical equipment also factor into the final figure. On a short urban trip, air conditioning alone can raise instant fuel consumption by 0.5 to 1 litre per 100 kilometres, an effect that is even more noticeable on small-displacement engines, which must work harder to compensate for the power drawn by the compressor.

The table below summarises the combined fuel consumption observed on the most economical petrol and petrol-hybrid models on the French market, across all segments.

These figures correspond to mixed use under the WLTP cycle, the current homologation standard. In real-world driving, a gap of 10 to 20 % upward is generally observed, more so in dense city traffic and in cold weather, when the combustion engine takes longer to reach its optimal operating temperature.

## The most economical petrol city cars for everyday use

The city car segment remains the preferred territory for the most economical engines, for a simple mechanical reason: a weight kept under one tonne and a small frontal area naturally limit energy needs. The latest non-plug-in hybrid city cars drop below 4 litres per 100 kilometres in the combined cycle, a level that brings them close to the best diesels while avoiding the maintenance constraints specific to that powertrain.

Among purely petrol engines, the new generation of small turbocharged three-cylinder units combines a reduced displacement with fine electronic injection management, keeping consumption under 5.5 litres per 100 kilometres in mixed use. ## Fuel consumption by segment: city car, compact car and petrol SUV

Beyond the powertrain, the vehicle's segment remains the most decisive factor. The larger and better equipped a model gets, the higher its consumption climbs, even with an identical powertrain technology.

### Why petrol SUVs use more fuel

A compact petrol SUV has a frontal area 15 to 20 % larger than an equivalent city car, which increases aerodynamic drag by the same margin at steady motorway speed. Combined with a weight typically 200 to 400 kilograms higher, this gap explains why a petrol SUV consumes on average 2 to 2.5 litres more per 100 kilometres than an equivalent city car in terms of powertrain.

### The lasting advantage of city cars and compacts

Compact cars, by contrast, retain an interesting balance between space and fuel economy, with combined consumption generally between 5 and 6.5 litres per 100 kilometres for the best petrol engines in the segment. ## How to reduce the fuel consumption of a petrol car

Beyond the choice of model, several levers can meaningfully reduce the consumption of a petrol vehicle already on the road. Correctly set tyre pressure, in line with the manufacturer's recommendations, limits rolling resistance, a factor that alone can shift consumption by 3 to 5 % when poorly adjusted.

Anticipating traffic while driving, with progressive acceleration and a wide reading of the road to avoid sudden braking, also measurably reduces consumption in urban use. Finally, regularly servicing the air filter and spark plugs keeps engine efficiency at its optimal level, whereas a poorly maintained engine can burn up to 10 % more fuel.

Unnecessary carried weight also plays a role that is often underestimated. Removing loads from the boot that have no everyday purpose there, such as a permanently mounted bike rack or heavy tools, lightens the vehicle and reduces the energy needed for every acceleration. On the motorway, holding a steady speed around 110 to 120 km/h rather than 130 km/h can cut consumption by nearly a litre per 100 kilometres, since aerodynamic drag rises exponentially with speed.

## Petrol, hybrid or electric: which choice consumes less

The choice between a conventional petrol engine, a non-plug-in hybrid and a fully electric vehicle depends above all on annual mileage and available charging conditions. For mostly urban use with short, frequent trips, the petrol hybrid often remains the best compromise between fuel economy, range and ease of use, without any charging constraint.
