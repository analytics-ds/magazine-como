The **most fuel-efficient car** question is a sensitive one as fuel prices keep rising. The ranking depends heavily on the segment considered and the type of use: a Japanese hybrid city car and a diesel SUV covering long distances do not answer the same needs, even though both aim for efficiency. This guide details the most economical models and engines by category, compares petrol, diesel and hybrid, and gives concrete benchmarks for cutting fuel consumption day to day.

## Ranking of the most fuel-efficient cars by category

Consumption levels vary enormously depending on a vehicle's size and engine. Among city cars, recent non-plug-in hybrids drop below 4.5 L/100 km on the mixed cycle, a result hard to match for a classic petrol or diesel engine of the same output. Among compacts and saloons, the gap between engine types narrows slightly but remains significant in favour of hybrids.

SUVs, being heavier and less aerodynamic, logically post higher consumption than city cars of similar build. A compact hybrid SUV typically sits between 5 and 6 L/100 km, against 6.5 to 8 L/100 km for an equivalent non-hybrid petrol version. The table below sums up market order-of-magnitude figures for each segment.

| Segment | Most efficient engine | Estimated mixed consumption |
|---|---|---|
| City car | Non-plug-in hybrid | 4.0 to 4.5 L/100 km |
| Compact / saloon | Non-plug-in hybrid | 4.3 to 5.0 L/100 km |
| Compact SUV | Plug-in hybrid (regular electric use) | 1.5 to 2.0 L/100 km |
| Compact SUV | Non-plug-in hybrid | 5.0 to 6.0 L/100 km |
| Saloon / diesel SUV | Recent diesel, motorway use | 4.5 to 5.5 L/100 km |

These figures remain averages observed in real mixed use. A **plug-in hybrid** can show a very low consumption figure if the battery is recharged daily, but it quickly approaches, or even exceeds, that of a classic hybrid once recharging becomes occasional.

## Petrol, diesel or hybrid: which engine consumes the least

The choice between [diesel or petrol](/en/blog/diesel-or-petrol/) is the first question to settle before even comparing models against each other. On motorways and over long distances, diesel keeps a real edge thanks to its generous low-rev torque and more efficient combustion at steady speed. A recent Euro 6d diesel can use 15 to 20 percent less than an equivalent petrol engine under these conditions.

In town and on short trips, that advantage largely reverses. Diesel struggles to reach optimal operating temperature over short distances, which degrades both its real consumption and the longevity of its emissions system. It is precisely in this urban context that the non-plug-in hybrid takes the lead: energy recovery under braking and phases of pure electric driving at low speed noticeably lower average consumption.

The plug-in hybrid is a special case. On trips covered by its electric range, fuel consumption can drop to zero. But once the battery is empty, its extra weight compared with a classic hybrid penalises its combustion-engine consumption, sometimes to the point of exceeding that of a conventional petrol car. The choice between a classic [best hybrid car](/en/blog/best-hybrid-car/) and a plug-in hybrid therefore mainly depends on how regularly the battery is recharged.

## Real-world consumption vs official WLTP figures

The consumption figures published by manufacturers come from the WLTP homologation cycle, carried out in a laboratory under controlled conditions. These values are useful for comparing models against each other, but they frequently diverge from the consumption observed in daily use.

Several factors explain this gap: driving style, vehicle load, use of air conditioning, trip profile, and outside temperature. In practice, the gap between WLTP and real-world consumption typically sits between 10 and 25 percent for a classic combustion engine, and can exceed 40 percent for a plug-in hybrid driven without regular recharging.

To estimate a realistic fuel budget, it is therefore better to rely on real-world driver feedback rather than the spec sheet alone. Owner forums and long-term tests published by specialist outlets generally offer figures closer to reality than official homologation.

## How to reduce fuel consumption day to day

Regardless of the model chosen, several driving habits can noticeably reduce real-world consumption, sometimes by 10 to 15 percent without changing vehicle.

- **Anticipate traffic**: easing off the accelerator gradually rather than braking at the last moment limits energy losses and reduces brake wear.
- **Check tyre pressure**: underinflation of 0.5 bar can increase consumption by 2 to 4 percent, on top of accelerating tyre wear.
- **Limit load and drag**: an empty roof rack or an overloaded boot adds weight and worsens aerodynamics, especially at motorway speed.
- **Stick to maintenance intervals**: a clogged air filter or an overdue oil change can degrade consumption by several percentage points, a point covered in our guide on [when to change the timing belt](/en/blog/when-to-change-timing-belt/).
- **Turn off the engine during long stops**: beyond thirty seconds stationary, the stop-start function or a manual shutdown limits unnecessary consumption.

These habits apply to every engine type, including electric cars, where anticipating regenerative braking directly affects real-world range, a topic covered in our article on the [best electric car](/en/blog/best-electric-car/) on the market.

Its recent hybrid engines drop below 5 L/100 km on the mixed cycle, a notable result for a compact saloon that offers a higher trim and equipment level than the most economical mainstream city cars on the market.

For drivers ready to consider going electric, the Mercedes-Benz EQ range removes fuel consumption altogether, at the cost of rethinking charging habits, a topic covered in our guide on [electric car charging prices](/en/blog/electric-car-charging-price/). The choice between an efficient combustion engine, a hybrid and a full electric mainly depends on annual mileage and access to regular charging.
